## Focus Areas
Some sample focus areas you can explore include: 

**Adapting to Changes in Sensory Perception**
- An elderly person may have varying sensory experiences day to day. They may feel too hot or too coo, have difficulty sensing the ground through footwear, experience changes in grip, or become sensitive to the taste and texture of food, drinks , and medications.
- Meaning, sensory needs and preferences are highly individual which makes it difficult for a product to provide the same level of accommodation to everyone in the same way. For example, a material, texture, temperature, or level of support that is comfortable for one person may be uncomfortable or impractical for another. There can also be design considerations to be made to attain to certain needs such as added cushioning may improve comfort while reducing feedback from the ground, or an easy-to-grip surface may introduce a texture that another person finds unpleasant.
- Therefore, there is a need to design adaptable products or experiences that better accommodate individual and changing sensory needs, while allowing users to maintain comfort, function, and control in everyday activities.

**Checking Sensory Aids Before Use to Prevent Discomfort**
- Sensory aids, such as hearing aids, are designed to support users. However, if they are not functioning or adjusted correctly they can create uncomfortable sensory experiences. For example, if a hearing aid is not set correctly in a user's ear or plays an unpleasant sound it creates discomfort.
- Additionally, an older adult with communication or cognitive difficulties may not be able to clearly identify or communicate a discomfort issue. Meaning, a caregiver may not realize that something is wrong.
- There is a opportunity to develop ways of checking, testing, or adjusting sensor aids before they are used. This can reduced reliance on user feedback and preventing avoidable discomfort where possible.

**Recognizing and Responding to Sensory Discomfort**
- Older adults may not always be able to recognize or communicate the source of sensory discomfort. Meaning, someone may become restless or agitated because they are too hot, too cold, thirsty, overwhelmed by noise, or otherwise uncomfortable without being able to communicate what is causing the problem to someone who can help them.
- This can make it difficult for caregivers, family members, or healthcare professionals to determine what the person needs. Additionally, the resulting behaviour may be interpreted as a behavioural problem when it may actually reflect an uncomfortable sensory experience or an unmet need.
- There is an opportunity to develop solutions that assist in recognizing potential sources of sensory discomfort and support and appropriate response. This could include reminders for needs such as drinking, way of identifying changes in the environment, or methods that help individuals communicate discomfort when traditional verbal or written communication is difficult.
